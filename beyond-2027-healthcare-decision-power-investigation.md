# Beyond 2027 — Who Will Control Healthcare Decisions?

**Arabic title:** من سيمتلك سلطة القرار في منظومة الرعاية الصحية والتأمين الصحي بعد 2027؟  
**Record type:** Cross-signal strategic investigation / emerging-theme analysis  
**Research cut-off:** 23 November 2026  
**Geographic scope:** GCC, with UAE as the primary evidence case and Saudi Arabia as the second developing comparison  
**Time horizon:** 2027–2030+, with 2027 treated as an observation and transition point, not a forecast boundary  
**Status:** Initial evidence synthesis — working investigation, not a final forecast  
**Confidence:** High for the existence of several regulatory and institutional developments described below; moderate for the interpretation that decision rights are being redistributed; low / not established for any claim that AI agents have become independent decision authorities across GCC healthcare or health insurance.

---

## 1. Central question

**Who will control healthcare decisions beyond 2027?**

This investigation asks who will hold effective authority over decisions across the patient, clinical, insurance and operational layers as AI systems and agents become more capable and more deeply embedded in healthcare and health-insurance workflows.

The question is not simply whether AI will replace doctors, insurers or administrators. It is whether decision power will be divided among actors who control different stages of a decision:

- who supplies and controls the information;
- who interprets it and recommends an action;
- who is authorised to approve, reject or override;
- who executes the resulting workflow;
- who can challenge the outcome; and
- who remains legally and operationally accountable.

**Working proposition to test:** Beyond 2027, healthcare decision power may become increasingly unbundled. Information, recommendation, authorisation, execution and accountability may sit with different actors rather than one decision-maker controlling the entire process.

This is an analytical proposition, not an established market outcome.

## 2. Five investigation questions

### Q1 — Will patient power increase, or will intelligent systems gain more influence over patient choices?

AI-enabled interfaces may help patients understand symptoms, compare insurance products, identify provider networks, prepare for consultations and navigate administrative steps. However, greater access to recommendations does not automatically mean greater patient agency.

A system that ranks options, filters information or determines which choices are presented may influence the decision even when the patient formally makes the final selection.

**Test:** Does AI increase the patient's ability to understand, compare, question and choose, or does it create dependence on an intermediary whose ranking logic, commercial incentives and data access are not transparent?

**Evidence already documented:** UAE-HI-005 records UAE-facing AI-mediated insurance-distribution interfaces, including provider-described advisory and comparison capabilities. The record distinguishes customer decision support from regulated underwriting, coverage or claims authority. Adoption scale, recommendation quality and customer outcomes remain unverified.

### Q2 — Will the physician remain the final clinical decision-maker in every case?

The relevant distinction is between AI supporting clinical work, AI performing a defined clinical activity under a regulated pathway, and AI exercising broad independent clinical authority.

On 20 October 2026, the Department of Health – Abu Dhabi published its AI Clinician Framework. It sets a pathway for research and assessment of AI systems proposed to perform specified clinical activities that would otherwise require a licensed clinician. Competence is assessed against the particular activity; wider scope requires stronger evidence. The framework includes safeguards such as physician oversight, accountability and referral to a physician when needed. It establishes a basis for future policies and standards; it does not demonstrate that AI clinicians have already received general authorisation to practise independently.

**Test:** Which clinical tasks may be delegated, under what evidence threshold, with what level of supervision, and who may stop or override the system?

**Boundary:** The AI Clinician Framework concerns clinical activities. It must not be treated as evidence that AI systems have been authorised to adjudicate insurance claims or determine coverage.

### Q3 — Will insurers retain control over financing and coverage decisions?

Insurers remain regulated entities with contractual and legal obligations. AI may change how evidence is assembled, policy rules are applied, cases are prioritised and exceptions are routed, without necessarily transferring the insurer's formal decision responsibility.

The UAE Central Bank's February 2026 guidance on responsible AI/ML use by Licensed Financial Institutions includes insurance providers and treats insurance claims as an example of a high-impact decision. Its risk-based approach addresses human oversight, consumer protection, explanations, review and institutional governance. The guidance does not establish a blanket requirement for prior human approval of every claim outcome.

UAE-HI-009 separately records Article 101 of Federal Decree-Law No. 48 of 2023: the insurance company must process claims in accordance with the policy and applicable legislation, issue a decision on each claim, and provide written reasons when a claim is rejected wholly or partly. Internal delegation and the exact operational division between insurer, TPA, technology provider and AI system remain case-specific questions.

**Test:** Does automation change only the production of a decision, or does it also change who has practical authority to approve, reject, explain, reverse and remedy it?

### Q4 — Will AI agents become independent intermediaries between institutions?

An AI agent may be able to coordinate a multi-step workflow across a patient, provider, insurer, TPA, pharmacy or other service. That capability is not equivalent to independent legal authority.

For this investigation, distinguish three levels:

| Level | Capability | Authority question |
|---|---|---|
| Assistant | Retrieves information and proposes options | Who chooses? |
| Delegated agent | Executes specified steps within a defined mandate | Who granted the mandate, and who can revoke it? |
| Decision authority | Approves, refuses or materially changes a consequential outcome | What law, contract or licence authorises it, and who is accountable? |

**Test:** Are agents merely tools operating inside an institution's controls, or are they becoming a new intermediary layer that controls the customer relationship, transaction routing, workflow priority or access to services?

**Current evidence boundary:** UAE-HI-006 and UAE-HI-007 document human roles around claims-engine automation, AI transformation and AI-solution delivery, as well as a disclosed Agentic AI case-management deployment outside confirmed health-claims adjudication. The reviewed evidence does not establish a named UAE TPA production deployment in which an AI agent independently holds regulated claims authority. Compatibility claims by vendors are not proof of customer deployment.

### Q5 — How will regulators redistribute authority and accountability?

Regulators can define which tasks may be automated, which decisions require human review, what evidence is required before deployment, how consumers can challenge outcomes, and which institution remains responsible when technology is outsourced.

In the UAE, the Cabinet approved a National Policy for Advancing Digital Healthcare Services and Artificial Intelligence in the Healthcare Sector in May 2026 and approved the drafting of a Federal Law to regulate smart health applications and AI in healthcare. The announced law is intended to address development, licensing, accreditation, operation and use of AI-powered health systems, including health-data governance, safety and quality, legal liability, patient rights and federal-local coordination. The announcement of drafting must not be described as enactment or entry into force.

**Test:** Will responsibility remain concentrated in the licensed provider or insurer, or will enforceable duties become more explicit for developers, deployers, data controllers, operators, professional users and agent supervisors?

## 3. Evidence base — what is established now?

### A. UAE: decision authority is being examined at several distinct layers

| Evidence / development | What it establishes | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| CBUAE AI/ML Guidance Note, 11 February 2026 | Federal financial-sector expectations for responsible AI/ML use by LFIs, including insurers; risk-based human oversight and consumer-protection considerations; insurance claims are an example of high-impact decisions | That every claim denial requires prior human approval; that insurers have deployed autonomous claims agents market-wide |
| DoH Abu Dhabi Responsible AI Standard, October 2025 | Human-oversight, escalation, safety, monitoring and accountability requirements within its stated Abu Dhabi healthcare-ecosystem scope | A UAE-wide rule applying identically to every healthcare entity, insurer and TPA |
| DoH Abu Dhabi AI Clinician Framework, 20 October 2026 | A regulated research and assessment pathway for specified clinical activities potentially performed by AI systems, with activity-specific competence and safeguards | General independent clinical authority for AI; insurance adjudication authority |
| UAE Cabinet national digital-health and AI policy, May 2026 | National policy direction to expand digital and AI applications across preventive, curative, rehabilitative and operational healthcare; emphasis on infrastructure, workforce, ethics and data governance | Completed implementation, measured health outcomes or a fully operational federal AI-health law |
| UAE Cabinet decision to draft a federal AI-health law, May 2026 | Intent to establish a unified legal framework covering AI-health-system development, licensing, accreditation, operation, liability, safety, data and patient rights | An enacted law or final implementing rules |
| UAE-HI-004 — Daman document intelligence | Publicly disclosed AI document classification, validation and extraction in Daman workflows | Autonomous medical-necessity decisions, claim approval/rejection authority or independently measured performance |
| UAE-HI-005 — AI-mediated insurance distribution | Provider-described AI assistance and comparison at the customer-facing insurance interface | Market-wide adoption, independently validated recommendations or regulated insurer decision authority |
| UAE-HI-006 / 007 — TPA workforce and ownership | Human automation and AI-transformation roles; organisational separation between AI solution delivery and process ownership in a recruitment case; a separate disclosed Agentic AI case-management deployment | A named TPA production agent with verified health-claims decision authority and a publicly identified accountable operator |
| UAE-HI-009 — vertical integration and claims control | Audited ownership links among PureHealth, Daman, SEHA and Daman subsidiaries, plus a separate AI document-processing announcement | Unified group decision control, unrestricted data access, or measurable economic advantage |
| UAE-HI-010 — patient data flow | Distinct clinical HIE infrastructure and insurance-claims exchange pathways; role-based access and data-governance requirements | General insurer/TPA access to complete longitudinal patient records |

### B. Saudi Arabia: AI infrastructure and insurance policy are developing, but health-specific agent authority is unproven

The Saudi Insurance Authority and HUMAIN signed a cooperation agreement on 31 August 2026 to explore AI capabilities, secure access to insurance-sector data, data quality, advanced analytics, digital governance and potential priority use cases across the insurance sector.

This is a sector-level cooperation agreement. It does not, by itself, establish a live health-insurance AI deployment, an autonomous claims agent, or delegated decision authority.

The Saudi Insurance Authority also directed health insurers to offer an outpatient product without pre-approval requests from 1 November 2026. This is relevant to the allocation of friction and control in the patient–payer relationship, but it is a product and regulatory intervention, not evidence of AI adoption.

Existing KSA records in this Observatory provide the starting evidence base:
- KSA-HI-001 — Outpatient health-insurance product without pre-approval.
- KSA-HI-002 — Insurance Authority–HUMAIN AI cooperation.
- KSA-HI-003 — NPHIES clinical coding validation edits.
- KSA-HI-005 — Insurance workforce development and medical-claims training.

**Saudi test:** whether sector-wide AI/data cooperation produces health-specific systems, production workflows, measurable changes in claims or authorisation, and explicit allocation of decision and override rights.

### C. Other GCC markets: regulatory and institutional variation is a research constraint

A GCC scoping review of private health insurance identifies materially different institutional arrangements, including different combinations of prudential insurance regulators, health authorities and dedicated health-insurance regulators. This means the UAE's federal-plus-emirate structure cannot be projected onto Saudi Arabia, Bahrain, Qatar, Oman or Kuwait.

The available initial evidence is uneven:
- **Bahrain:** a national AI policy and adoption of the GCC AI ethics manual were announced in July 2025. Bahrain's health-data and insurance framework also contains specific provisions for electronic health records, health-information governance and coverage-provider data security. A current health-insurance-specific AI deployment or delegated decision-authority case has not yet been established in this investigation.
- **Qatar:** the national AI strategy and financial-sector AI guidance are relevant background, but a current health-insurance-specific AI decision workflow requires primary-source verification.
- **Oman:** the national AI programme and 2026 digital/legal developments provide a policy and data-governance context. Their implications for health-insurance decision rights require separate legal and operational verification.
- **Kuwait:** the available evidence is insufficient to make a defensible comparison of health-insurance AI authority, agent deployment or operational accountability.

**GCC conclusion at this stage:** there is not yet a sufficiently complete, comparable evidence base to rank the six markets by AI decision autonomy or regulatory readiness. Country-level gaps must remain visible rather than being filled by inference.

## 4. Three kinds of power to map

The investigation should not use “control” as a single undifferentiated concept. Every case should be mapped across these dimensions:

1. **Information Power:** who creates, holds, standardises, accesses and interprets the relevant data?
2. **Decision Power:** who recommends, authorises, refuses, overrides or appeals the decision?
3. **Execution Power:** who triggers the transaction, coordinates the workflow, communicates the outcome and completes the operational steps?

A fourth dimension cuts across all three:

4. **Accountability:** who must explain, correct, compensate for, report or remedy a harmful or unlawful outcome?

A party may have strong information power but no formal decision authority. An AI agent may have execution power under a limited mandate without being a legal decision-maker. A licensed insurer may retain legal responsibility even where a TPA or technology provider performs operational steps.

## 5. Competing scenarios for 2027–2030+

These are scenarios to monitor, not probability-weighted forecasts.

### Scenario A — Institution-controlled automation

Insurers and providers embed AI into existing operating models. AI increases speed and consistency, but institutions retain approval rights, governance, override mechanisms and accountability.

**Signals that would strengthen it:** named internal AI owners; documented decision matrices; human review for material cases; audit trails; clear vendor contracts; measurable automation within existing delegation structures.

### Scenario B — Platform and agent intermediation

Patient-facing or enterprise agents increasingly coordinate transactions across providers, insurers and administrators. The actor controlling the interface, identity, workflow and transaction routing may gain influence even without holding formal clinical or insurance authority.

**Signals that would strengthen it:** cross-institution agent integrations; delegated transaction mandates; persistent patient/enterprise agent identities; agent-to-agent protocols; evidence that customers follow agent recommendations or that institutions depend on third-party orchestration.

### Scenario C — Regulated, task-specific AI authority

Regulators permit AI systems to perform narrowly defined clinical or administrative activities after evidence-based assessment, with explicit limits, supervision, escalation and liability arrangements.

**Signals that would strengthen it:** enacted laws and implementing standards; activity-specific licences or approvals; published performance thresholds; incident reporting; formal override and appeal rights; named accountable operators.

### Scenario D — Concentrated ecosystem control

Integrated payer-provider groups or large technology/data platforms gain stronger influence because they control multiple points in the information, financing and delivery chain.

**Signals that would strengthen it:** verified control of data and operating platforms; related-party workflow integration; cross-entity decision systems; measurable shifts in referrals, utilisation, reimbursement or member routing.

**Counter-evidence to seek:** legal-entity separation, data-access restrictions, independent provider networks, competition rules, contractual limits and evidence that integrated ownership does not translate into operational decision control.

### Scenario E — Patient agency with AI as a contestable assistant

Patients gain better access to understandable information, comparison, consent controls, explanations and independent human review. AI remains a tool that supports rather than silently narrows patient choice.

**Signals that would strengthen it:** accessible patient data and consent tools; transparent recommendation criteria; clear human escalation; portable records; meaningful appeal outcomes; evidence that patients can reject AI-mediated pathways without losing access.

## 6. What would falsify the central proposition?

The working proposition is that decision power may become unbundled. It should be revised or rejected if evidence shows that:

- institutions retain substantially the same decision rights and AI only automates clerical preparation;
- AI recommendations do not materially influence patient, clinical or payer outcomes;
- agents remain narrow workflow tools without cross-institution authority or persistent intermediary roles;
- regulation consistently keeps consequential authority with existing licensed actors and does not create new delegated roles;
- data access and interoperability remain too fragmented to support cross-entity decision orchestration; or
- operational economics make human-led coordination more effective than agent-led alternatives in material workflows.

Evidence of AI use is not enough to confirm a redistribution of power. We need evidence that the allocation of authority, practical influence or accountability has changed.

## 9. Initial conclusion — deliberately provisional

The evidence does not support the claim that AI has already taken control of healthcare or health-insurance decisions across the UAE or GCC.

It does support a narrower and more consequential finding: regulators, insurers, healthcare providers and technology partners are beginning to define how AI can participate in clinical and insurance workflows, while questions of operational ownership, decision rights, data access and accountability remain unevenly visible.

The most defensible expectation for 2027 is therefore not “AI replaces the decision-maker.” It is that more decisions will be prepared, influenced or executed through AI-enabled systems, while the boundaries between recommendation, authorisation, execution and accountability become increasingly important.

**The decisive question is not only who makes the decision. It is who can influence it, who can stop it, who can challenge it, and who is answerable for its consequences.**
